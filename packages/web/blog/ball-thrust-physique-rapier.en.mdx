Release the right arrow: the sphere keeps rolling. Push toward an obstacle: it gets blocked or bounces. In Ball Thrust, these reactions are not animations written to convince the viewer. They come from the Rapier physics world.

Before asking an agent to reach a target, we need to check that the world responds correctly to its future actions. **This first slice is a manually controlled physics game, with no neural network, training loop or RL reward.** We start by making the task playable and measurable.

## An arena for understanding inertia

A cyan, 1 kg sphere moves inside a 12 × 12 m arena surrounded by four walls. Three fixed obstacles separate the start from the green beacon. The floor, walls and obstacles have real colliders; only the sphere is a dynamic body.

![Ball Thrust before any impulse: cyan sphere, three obstacles and green target.](/images/blog/ball-thrust/initial.png)

*Actual demo capture with seed 70. The camera observes the simulation; it does not move any physics body. The demo interface is currently in French.*

Click inside the arena or select it with Tab, then use:

| Control | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Arrow keys, WASD or ZQSD | Horizontal impulses |
| Two combined directions | Diagonal movement, without extra thrust magnitude |
| Space, even while a direction remains held | No-op: no new impulse |
| Releasing the keys | Also a no-op, not a brake |
| Opposite direction to motion | Counter-thrust to brake |
| Drag / scroll | Orbit / zoom without changing the control axes |

Up always means −z and right means +x, even after rotating the camera. Keyboard play is the supported control mode; a narrow layout is not a touch controller.

## An impulse is not a displacement

The world advances in fixed 1/60 s steps. While a direction is held, the controller delivers a 0.65 N·s impulse every six steps: 10 impulses per second, independent of keyboard repeat or rendering frequency.

An impulse changes momentum. For a mass of 1 kg, a horizontal 0.65 N·s impulse produces an immediate 0.65 m/s change in velocity along that axis, before contacts and damping take effect. It does not choose the next position. Rapier then resolves gravity, collisions, friction and restitution.

Diagonals are normalized: pressing two arrows does not double the energy of each push. With a zero direction, `thrust()` does nothing: it does not set velocity to zero. Contact friction and damping gradually dissipate motion. To stop near the target, you need to anticipate and apply the opposite direction.

Friction and restitution coefficients are 0.65 and 0.55 respectively; the sphere also uses linear damping of 0.25 and angular damping of 0.4. Continuous collision detection (CCD) and predictive contacts help limit tunneling at speed. These settings are not a substitute for contact tests.

## Rapier decides, R3F displays

`populateWorld()` creates bodies and colliders in the world provided by R3F's `Physics` component. That provider alone advances the browser simulation. The sphere mesh then copies the Rapier body's position and quaternion; no competing movement calculation runs in the renderer.

The HUD reads that same body at 10 Hz: three-dimensional position and velocity, speed, horizontal target distance and simulated time. A rendering cadence of 120 frames per second therefore does not imply 120 physics steps per second.

The target is reached when the sphere's center enters a horizontal radius of 0.7 m near floor height. The success message stays visible, but physics continues: reaching the target does not artificially freeze the sphere.

## Recreate the same starting state

The **Graine du monde** field accepts an integer from 0 to 4294967295. The seed determines the starting and target positions in two obstacle-free corners. With seed 70, the resting HUD position is approximately `(-4.32, 0.35, 4.44)` m.

Pressing **Réinitialiser** does not simply move the mesh back. Reset recreates the physics world: bodies, linear and angular velocities, orientation, solver contacts and step count all return to their initial state. The canvas and camera stay alive.

Two worlds with the same seed, Rapier version and actions at the same simulation steps follow the same trajectory in the tests. This is not a promise of identical trajectories across engine versions, or between two sessions where a human presses keys at roughly the same time.

## Verify before training

![The sphere on the green beacon, with the HUD reporting Cible atteinte.](/images/blog/ball-thrust/goal.png)

*The target was reached through keyboard input in a real browser. This shows that the task is playable; it does not measure a learned policy's performance.*

The 41 dedicated tests use the real Rapier/WASM engine and the same factory as the scene. They check, among other things:

- zero initial velocities and trajectory reproducibility after reset;
- impulses, diagonals and no-op without teleportation;
- floor support, dissipation and rebounds against each of the three obstacles;
- containment during 30 seconds of continuous thrust in each of the eight directions, using seeds 0, 70, 71 and 4294967295. A transient 3 cm tolerance is allowed for the contact solver, without position clamping.

To run them again:

```bash
pnpm --filter demo-ball-thrust test
pnpm --filter demo-ball-thrust typecheck
pnpm test
```

Validation of source commit `d79341e1665c6413992378ce775941d909b45d32` also checked controls, resets, the target, orbiting and a text error when WebGL is unavailable. On Apple M4, headed Chrome 155 with ANGLE Metal, a moving walkthrough measured an average RAF cadence of 120.01 FPS over 30 seconds, with a 9.2 ms inter-frame p95 and no console errors. This is a local RAF measurement, not GPU timing or a mobile performance promise.

## Next: an RL contract, not a more convincing animation

This slice does not yet provide a `TrainingEnv`, policy or reward. The next step needs bounded observations, explicit actions, a dense reward, distinct episode endings (`terminated` and `truncated`) and a seeded reset. The agent must control this same physics world, without letting the renderer take over the simulation.

For now, try reaching the target and then stopping there. If doing that at the keyboard already requires anticipating inertia, the future agent will have a genuine control problem to learn.
